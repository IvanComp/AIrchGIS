C HAMELEON: A Framework for Dynamic Selection
of Aggregation Strategies in Federated Learning
Anonymous Author(s)

Abstract—Federated Learning (FL) enables multiple clients to
collaboratively train a shared model without exposing their local
data. At each round, the server collects local model updates
from clients and aggregates them into a shared global model.
Several aggregation strategies exist, ranging from simple weight
averaging, such as FedAvg, to variants tailored to address specific
issues, such as unreliable clients or skewed data distributions.
System boundary conditions, however, rarely remain constant:
data distributions shift, clients drop in and out, computational
and communication resources fluctuate, and convergence can
become unstable across rounds. These factors influence which
aggregation strategy is best suited at a given point in training.
Yet most FL systems select a single strategy before training
begins and keep it fixed throughout, even though a strategy that
works well early on may no longer be the best choice later. This
work presents Chameleon, a runtime adaptation framework
for selecting performance-oriented FL strategies. After each
communication round, a server-side adaptation manager observes
runtime metrics and boundary conditions and selects the strategy
for the next round. We model strategy selection as a runtime
decision problem over a finite set of compatible FL strategies. We
study multiple adaptation policies, including random, predictorbased, Bayesian optimization-based, and LLM-driven selection.
We evaluate Chameleon across diverse FL settings, including
IID and non-IID data, heterogeneous clients, varying participation rates, and different learning tasks. Results show...

I. I NTRODUCTION
Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed machine learning
(ML) paradigm in which multiple clients collaboratively train
a shared model without exposing their local data [1], [2].
Google introduced this paradigm in 2017 to solve a concrete
problem on smartphone devices: improving keyboard nextword prediction required training on personal chat data, which
is sensitive in its own right and poses even greater risk when
centralized on a single location [2]. Instead of moving the
data, the computation runs on the device: each phone trains
the model locally on its own data, and only the resulting
model update is sent back to the server for aggregation.
In a typical FL process, the server broadcasts the current
global model to a subset of clients, each client performs local
training on its own data, and the server aggregates the resulting
updates to refine the global model’s weights. This last step is
referred to as model aggregation: the server aggregates the
locally trained models, gradients, or weights to produce an
updated global model [1]. Hence, model aggregation is not a
secondary operation but a key step: the accuracy, reliability,
and the convergence speed of the resulting global model
depend directly on the chosen aggregation strategy [1]–[3].

A recent survey [3] analyzes 201 studies on model aggregation strategies in FL and organizes them according to how
client updates are coordinated, collected, and combined into
the global model. The survey identifies four main families:
(i) synchronous aggregation, where the server updates the
global model after receiving the expected client contributions;
(ii) asynchronous aggregation, where this synchronization requirement is relaxed so that updates can be incorporated as
they become available; (iii) hierarchical aggregation, where
aggregation is distributed across intermediate levels, such as
edge or regional servers; and (iv) robust aggregation, where
the aggregation rule is designed to tolerate unreliable, lowquality, or malicious client updates. This taxonomy reflects the
main challenges that aggregation strategies aim to mitigate. In
particular, FL aggregation is affected by statistical heterogeneity, since clients may train on non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data; by system communication
bottlenecks, since clients may differ in availability, bandwidth,
or computation time; and by secure aggregation requirements,
which aim to combine client updates while limiting the impact
of unreliable or adversarial participants [3]. Different families
address these challenges with different trade-offs: synchronous
strategies provide a simple and stable round-based protocol
but may suffer from heterogeneous or delayed updates; asynchronous and hierarchical strategies improve timeliness and
scalability but introduce stale updates or additional coordination; robust strategies improve reliability and security but often
add computational or communication overhead.
Accordingly, aggregation strategies are typically designed
to mitigate one or more of these challenges, but each design
choice introduces trade-offs. Synchronous strategies provide
a simple, stable round-based protocol but can be sensitive
to stragglers and heterogeneous updates. Asynchronous and
hierarchical strategies improve timeliness or scalability, but
may introduce stale updates, additional coordination, or architectural complexity. Robust strategies improve reliability
and security, but often add computational or communication
overhead. Therefore, the choice of an aggregation strategy
depends on the dominant challenge in a given FL setting,
rather than on a single “silver bullet” method.
Within this design space, this paper focuses on performanceoriented strategies that can be exchanged within the same
round-based FL training loop. This scope includes synchronous aggregation rules and server-side optimization strategies, which preserve the same execution structure but differ in
how they combine, smooth, or regularize client updates. F E DAVG [1] is the standard baseline that aggregates client models

via a weighted average proportional to each client’s local data.
F EDAVG M [4] adds server-side momentum to smooth noisy
update trajectories, F ED P ROX [5] constrains local training to
reduce client drift, and adaptive server optimizers such as
F EDA DAM [6] stabilize learning when the magnitudes and
directions of client updates vary across rounds.
Despite this variety, FL systems commonly adopt a static
strategy: a single aggregation or server optimization method is
selected before training begins and used throughout all rounds.
This design assumes that the same strategy remains appropriate for the entire training process. In practice, this assumption
is often restrictive. FL systems are dynamic: clients may join
or drop out, computational resources may vary, communication
costs may change, and the model may move through different
learning phases. A strategy that performs well during early
exploration may be less effective when convergence slows;
similarly, a strategy designed for heterogeneous updates may
become useful only after client drift becomes visible.
This paper is motivated by the following observation: the
best FL strategy is not necessarily fixed, but may depend
on the current state of the federation. For instance, F EDAVG
may be sufficient when clients are homogeneous and updates
are stable, while F ED P ROX may become preferable when
statistical or system heterogeneity causes client drift. Similarly,
adaptive server optimizers such as F EDA DAM can be useful
when convergence becomes noisy or unstable. These examples
suggest that selecting an aggregation strategy can be treated
as a runtime adaptation problem.
We present Chameleon, a framework for selecting runtime
aggregation strategies in FL. Rather than fixing a single
strategy before training, Chameleon uses a server-side adaptation manager to observe the system’s evolving state and
select the strategy for the next round. The decision relies on
information already available at the server, including predictive performance, round duration, communication behavior,
client participation, and indicators of statistical or system
heterogeneity. We study several adaptation policies for this
decision problem: a random policy as a lower-bound reference,
a predictor-based policy that maps runtime features to candidate strategies, a Bayesian optimization policy that balances
exploration and exploitation, and an LLM-driven policy that
combines runtime observations with prior knowledge about
the assumptions and strengths of each aggregation strategy.
We evaluate Chameleon across multiple FL configurations,
including non-IID data distribution, varying federation sizes,
and heterogeneous client characteristics, comparing adaptive
selection policies against static single-strategy baselines to
assess whether runtime adaptation can better adapt to the
changing conditions of the FL process.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
• We formulate the selection of FL aggregation strategies
as a runtime adaptation problem driven by performance
metrics and system observations.
• We introduce Chameleon, a framework that dynamically selects the aggregation strategy for the next FL
round.

II. P RELIMINARIES
A. Federated Learning in a Nutshell
In traditional machine learning (ML), training data is typically centralized on cloud servers to develop a single predictive
model [7]. Rising privacy concerns and regulatory constraints
have reduced organizations’ willingness to share sensitive
data [2], leading to data silos where information remains
isolated for protection [8]. Federated learning (FL) addresses
this by enabling collaborative model training across distributed
clients or devices, without moving their local data to a central
server [1], [2]. Google introduced the original formulation in
2017, motivated initially by applications such as next-word
prediction on smartphone keyboards [1]. Since then, the field
has grown rapidly, driven by successful applications across
domains such as healthcare, finance, and industry [9].
Figure 1 shows an overview of the general FL framework.
Each communication round follows four steps. 1 Clients
download the current global model parameters from the server
and use them to initialize their local “snapshot”, replacing
whatever local weights they held from the previous round.
2 Each client then trains this snapshot on its own local
dataset, typically running several epochs of stochastic gradient
descent (or a similar optimizer), and updates its local model
independently of every other client. 3 Once local training
finishes, clients send their updated model parameters or the
computed gradients back to the server. 4 The server collects
the contributions from participating clients and aggregates
them, using an aggregation strategy, an information fusion
algorithm such as F EDAVG [1], into a global model for the
next round. The process repeats over multiple rounds until a

stopping criterion is met, such as reaching a target accuracy
or a fixed number of communications rounds.
B. Aggregation Strategies and Baselines
The aggregation phase defines how the server combines
client contributions into the next global model [3].
The most common strategy is F EDAVG [1], which computes
a data-weighted average of client models. Although F EDAVG
is simple and widely adopted, it may be suboptimal when
clients are heterogeneous, updates are noisy, convergence
is unstable, or the system objective goes beyond average
accuracy.
Several aggregation and server-side optimization strategies
have therefore been proposed. In this paper, we use the
term strategy broadly to denote any method that affects how
the global model is updated from client contributions. This
includes classical aggregation rules, server-side optimizers,
personalization methods, robustness mechanisms, and taskspecific FL baselines. Table ?? summarizes the strategies and
baselines considered in Chameleon, grouped by objective
and integration status.
a) General-purpose and optimization-oriented aggregation: F EDAVG [1] is the canonical FL aggregation rule and
serves as the main reference point for federated training.
F EDAVG M [4] extends F EDAVG with server-side momentum, smoothing global update directions across rounds and
improving convergence when client updates are noisy or nonIID. Adaptive server optimizers such as F EDA DAM, F EDA DA GRAD , and F EDYOGI [6] treat aggregation as a server-side
optimization process. F EDA DAM improves stability through
adaptive moment estimates, F EDA DAGRAD adapts learning
rates according to accumulated update history, and F EDYOGI
limits overly aggressive adaptation when client updates are
heterogeneous.
b) Heterogeneity and client drift: Several methods address statistical or system heterogeneity. F ED P ROX [5] modifies local training to limit how far client models drift
from the global model, making it useful when clients have
non-IID data or different computational capabilities. F ED N OVA [14] normalizes client updates to reduce inconsistencies
caused by clients performing different amounts of local work.
F ED BN [11] keeps batch normalization statistics local to
each client, improving robustness under feature shift. H ETERO FL [20] supports clients with different computational and
communication capacities by leveraging heterogeneous model
structures. F J ORD [18] uses ordered dropout to adapt model
complexity to client resources, while D EPTH FL [19] enables
clients to train models with different depths according to their
capabilities.
c) Fairness, personalization, and adaptation: Some
methods change the objective from learning a single
global model to improving fairness or personalization. Q F EDAVG [10] gives greater influence to clients with poorer
local performance, improving fairness across the federation.
F ED P ER [16] separates shared and personalized layers, allowing each client to keep part of the model local. F E -

D R EP [17] learns a shared representation while maintaining
client-specific prediction heads. F ED M ETA [12] applies metalearning to support fast adaptation to client-specific tasks.
F ED MLB [13] introduces multi-level branched regularization
to balance shared learning and personalization. MOON [21]
uses model-contrastive learning to improve representation
quality under non-IID data. F LOCO [23] studies federated
learning over connected modes, exploiting relationships between different model solutions instead of forcing all clients
toward a single optimum.
d) Communication and computation efficiency.: Other
methods focus on reducing the cost of FL training. F ED PARA [15] uses low-rank parameterization to reduce the
number of transmitted parameters and improve communication
efficiency. DASHA [22] combines communication compression with variance reduction to support efficient optimization
under limited communication. TAMUNA [24] addresses acceleration, compression, and partial participation, making it
relevant when only a subset of clients can participate or when
communication resources are constrained.
e) Task-specific, model-specific, and domain-specific
baselines.: Some baselines are designed for specific learning
tasks or model families rather than general-purpose aggregation. F ED PFT [25] targets one-shot FL with foundation
models. F ED S TAR [26] focuses on semi-supervised federated self-training. F EDVSSL [27] addresses federated selfsupervised video learning. F EDWAV 2V EC 2 [28] is designed
for federated speech recognition. H F ED XGB OOST [29] targets
federated gradient boosting trees rather than neural network
models. S TATAVG [30] is a domain-specific method for nonIID intrusion detection. These methods are relevant in their
intended domains, but they are not directly comparable in a
common image-classification FL setting without changing the
task, model family, or evaluation protocol.
f) Robustness, fraudulent clients, diagnostics, and benchmarking.: A separate group of methods addresses robustness,
debugging, or evaluation rather than standard performance optimization. FLANDERS [32] targets robustness against model
poisoning attacks, making it relevant when clients may behave
maliciously or submit fraudulent updates. F ED D EBUG [31]
focuses on debugging and fault localization in FL systems,
helping identify problematic clients or training failures. NIIDB ENCH [33] is a benchmark suite for evaluating FL methods
under non-IID data silos. These baselines are valuable for
analyzing reliability and robustness, but they are not included
in the current performance-oriented selector because their
primary objective is not to improve standard non-adversarial
training performance.
